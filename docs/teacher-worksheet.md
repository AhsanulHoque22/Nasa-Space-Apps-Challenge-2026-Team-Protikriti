# Plan a safe Marswalk: classroom worksheet

For students about 12 and up, 45 minutes, one computer per pair. Open the Martian Map site and
work through the *Walkthrough* panel first (five steps, five minutes). Then answer below.

Everything here uses the app's own planning rules. Those rules are the team's assumptions, not
NASA values, and a rover can sit on a slope a person could not. The point is the reasoning.

## The rules the app uses

- Ground steeper than **15°** is treated as impassable.
- Walking pace is capped at **3.3 km/h** in a spacesuit.
- A spacewalk (EVA) is at most **8 hours**, and **1 hour** is kept in reserve.
- The walk home is **20% slower** than the walk out, because the crew is tired.
- Each science stop takes **20 minutes**.

## Questions

1. **Slope.** A 15° slope rises how many metres over 100 metres of ground? (Hint: a calculator,
   `tan 15°`.) Find a red hatched area in Jezero. Why is it hatched?
2. **Pace.** How long does it take to walk 3.3 km at the capped pace? Plan a route of about 3 km
   in the app. Is the time it shows close to your estimate? Why might it be a little different?
3. **Out and back.** You may be away 8 − 1 = 7 hours. If you walk out for *t* hours, you need
   *t* hours out plus 1.2 *t* hours back. What is the longest straight walk out you could make on
   flat ground, with no stops? Give it in hours and in kilometres.
4. **A stop costs time.** Now add one 20 minute science stop at the far end. Does the longest
   walk out get shorter or longer? By how much?
5. **Test it.** Plan a route of about 12 km in the app. What does the safety card say? Where does
   it mark "Fails here", and how many minutes short is the crew?
6. **See it.** Open *Planning tools* and switch on *Walking range*. What does the dashed white
   line show? Does it match your answer to question 3?
7. **Block the way.** Switch on *Mark hazard* and click on your route. Does the route go around?
   How much longer is the walk?
8. **Be honest.** The app says its map cannot see boulders. Open *Checked against Perseverance's
   real drive*. In what way was the map too optimistic? Why does that matter for a real crew?

## Answer key

1. About **27 m** for every 100 m of ground (`tan 15° ≈ 0.268`). The hatching marks ground the
   planner will not cross.
2. 3.3 km takes **1 hour**. The app's time differs a little because the route is not perfectly
   straight, and the pace slows on climbs (it is Tobler's hiking function, scaled, then capped).
3. *t* + 1.2 *t* = 2.2 *t* ≤ 7 hours, so *t* ≤ **3.18 hours**, which is about **10.5 km**.
4. **Shorter.** *t* + 20 min + 1.2 *t* ≤ 7 h gives *t* ≈ 3.03 h, about **10.0 km**: the stop costs
   roughly half a kilometre of range.
5. Expect **NO-GO**: 12 km out is 3.64 hours, and out plus the slower walk home is 8.0 hours, over
   the 7 you may use. The marker sits where the crew can no longer get home in time. (Your exact
   figures depend on the route the app chooses.)
6. The farthest ground you can reach and still get home in time. On flat ground it is near 10.5 km
   from the start; the map's slopes and the site edge change that.
7. It should detour. The panel reports the extra distance and time. A small sidestep may add
   nothing measurable.
8. At the points where the rover's own tilt sensor read above 15°, the map showed gentler ground.
   The map is too coarse to see rocks and small scarps, so the red hatching is a minimum, not a
   guarantee. A real crew would check finer images before leaving.

## For the teacher

- Works offline after one visit online (see the README), useful for slow school connections.
- Nothing is saved or sent; no account is needed.
- Extension: change one assumption (say, a slower pace) and predict how the 10.5 km figure moves.
