# NASA splits Mars cargo, never one lander

NASA never plans a crewed Mars surface stay around one lander that carries the habitat, the reactors and all the food. Design Reference Architecture 5.0 lands two ~40 t-payload vehicles per crew. The cargo lander (DAV) lands about two years early with the fission reactor, two ISRU plants, the ascent vehicle and **4,500 kg** of consumables. The habitat lander (SHAB) brings the **21.5 t** habitat, rovers, a second power system and just **1,500 kg** of consumables ([DRA 5.0, Table 5-1, p.37](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). Later Evolvable Mars Campaign studies used smaller 18–27 t landers, three to five per expedition, with every reactor on the first lander ([Polsgrove 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160004402); [Rucker 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550)). Fission wins over solar on landed mass: **9,154 kg** of Kilopower (4 + 1 spare, 35 kWe) against **11,713 kg** of solar plus batteries at Jezero. NASA carries food rather than growing it on any mission shorter than about 4.8 years. It makes oxygen and water from recycling (98% water recovery on ISS) and ISRU. Its main reserve is a full parallel food stock (**7,940 kg**) kept in orbit, not on the surface. For the game, the blocked "3 reactors + enough food on one 40 t lander" case becomes feasible with NASA's own tools: a second cargo lander, food that only has to last until the next ship plus a margin, a lighter Mars menu, and right-sized ISRU. Each of these is quantified below.

## Two pre-landed vehicles carry what one 40 t lander cannot

DRA 5.0 launches both cargo elements one opportunity (**~26 months**) before the crew. They arrive about 8 months after launch. The DAV lands on its own, deploys a ~30 kWe reactor and makes ascent oxygen and crew consumables. Surface power deployment and a fully fueled ascent vehicle are go/no-go conditions for launching the crew ([DRA 5.0, pp.3, 33, 51–52](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). The SHAB waits semi-dormant in Mars orbit, and the crew rides it down, so the habitat lander is also the crew lander. Each lander delivers **40.4 t** of payload from a 109.7 t entry mass ([DRA 5.0 EDL table, p.33](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)).

| Item (DRA 5.0 Table 5-1, kg) | Habitat lander | DAV (cargo) lander |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat + growth | 16,500 + 5,000 | – |
| Pressurized rovers (2) + growth | 8,000 + 1,600 | – |
| Stationary power system | 7,800 | 7,800 |
| ISRU plants (2) | – | 1,130 |
| Crew consumables | 1,500 | 4,500 |
| Science, drill, small rovers, cache, rover power | – | 5,000 |
| **Surface systems total** | **40,400** | **18,430** (+ ~21.5 t ascent stages) |

The consumables split is exactly **75/25**: three quarters pre-landed, one quarter with the crew. The habitat lander is already full at 40.4 t, so NASA does not try to add food to it. EMC studies found that 40 t landers are hard to pack anyway, because pressurized modules are bulky and low in density. They counted **five landers at 18 t, three at 27 t, or two at 40 t** for a 500-sol mission ([Polsgrove 2016, pp.1–2](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160004402)). Rucker's EMC manifest puts power and cargo on lander 1, MAV and ISRU on lander 2, mixed cargo and consumables on lander 3, and habitat and crew on lander 4. Later expeditions need only three landers, because they reuse infrastructure at the same site within 1 km ([Rucker 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550); [slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160011275)).

Pre-deployment also serves as redundancy. The next crew's cargo lands before the first crew leaves. If the first DAV or SHAB fails, "the second set of cargo elements could be used, thus potentially preventing loss of the mission or of the crew" ([DRA 5.0 §6.3.1, p.51](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). For the game, the ship landing at sol 742–804 is therefore a planned part of the supply chain, and NASA would count it in the plan.

## Fission sized at 26–35 kWe beats solar, which must survive a 120-sol storm

Propellant production, not the crew, sets the power level. DRA 5.0's ISRU plant needs **25–26 kWe continuous**, while the crew phase needs 17–20 kWe, of which the habitat takes **12.1 kWe** ([DRA 5.0 §5.4.1, §6.6](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). Rucker's 4-crew EMC budget gives a pre-crew peak of **26,355 W** (ISRU 19,700 plus MAV 6,655) and a crew-phase peak of **31,099 W**. That crew peak includes an *optional* 9,544 W science lab, and crew-phase keep-alive is only **14,829 W** ([Rucker 2016, Table 3](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550)). NASA met that budget with four 10 kWe Kilopower units "up to 35 kWe continuous" plus one spare. The five units total **8,769 kg**, or 9,154 kg with cables and controllers. Each unit is **1,754 kg** including 15% mass growth, and nearly a third of that is shielding. Units sit about 1 km from the habitat and last roughly 12 years, about three expeditions ([Rucker 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550)).

Solar costs more mass because it must survive dust storms. Rucker sizes the Jezero arrays for a **120-day storm at optical depth 5**, where diffuse light is ~30–40% of a clear day. Each lander carries four 12 m UltraFlex arrays. Lander 1 carries **5,611 kg**, including **3,168 kg** of Li-ion storage (165 Wh/kg, 60% depth of discharge), and landers 2–4 carry **2,034 kg** each, for **11,713 kg** in total. At Columbus Crater (29.5°S) the total rises to 12,679 kg ([Rucker 2016, Tables 6–8](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550); [slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160011275/downloads/20160011275.pdf)). In the storm case, power falls to **~11 kW** while still keeping the crew alive. DRA 5.0's solar option needed **~22.5 t**, including a 4,300 m², 7,800 kg thin-film array that the crew rolls out for a 50-day storm ([DRA 5.0 §6.6.3](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). Observed storms support those margins. At Viking Lander 1 (22°N), clear-day insolation is ~3.9 kWh/m² per sol, but at τ = 3.25 it falls to ~1.0 kWh/m², about 30% of clear ([Appelbaum & Flood 1989](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890018252)). The 2018 global storm reached **τ 10.8** and cut Opportunity from 645 to **22 Wh/sol** ([Planetary Society](https://www.planetary.org/articles/06-mer-update-opportunity-dust-storm-sleep)). Dust also costs ~0.2% of array output per day, and cleaning events cannot be relied on ([DRA 5.0 §6.6.6](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). Solar does improve over time. By the third expedition, arrays left by earlier landers add up to enough area to "accommodate a 120-sol dust storm with little disruption", although cumulative solar mass is then more than twice that of fission ([Rucker slides](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160011275)).

Two of the game's numbers are derived rather than stated. The figure of 8.75 kWe per unit is 35 kWe ÷ 4 working units (each unit is nominally 10 kWe). The ISRU plant mass of ~1,678 kg is back-calculated from Rucker's statement that a Columbus-site variant adds "about 40%, or 671 kg". Two smaller ISRU references exist. DRA 5.0's propellant-scale plant is **565.5 kg and 23.71 kWe each**, with two carried for redundancy ([DRA 5.0 Table 5-2, pp.40–41](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). Its consumables-only plant (atmospheric O2 plus water from 3%-water soil) is **722 kg at 4.92 kW** running 24 hours ([Addendum Table 3-22](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)).

## NASA carries food for any stay under about five years

The baseline is packaged food. ISS food rose from 1.83 to **2.39 kg per crewmember-day including packaging**, which is the game's figure. NASA's early-Mars low-moisture menu is lighter at **1.23 kg plus 0.28 kg packaging (1.51 kg/CM-d)**, and DRA plans at ~2.45 kg/CM-d for all consumables ([BVAD Rev2, pp.83, 126](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855); [DRA Addendum Table 5-10](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)). NASA's food-technology lead estimated **9,660 kg** of fully stored food for 6 crew over 1,000 days. That food needs a 5-year shelf life and "prepositioning may be required" ([Perchonok, NTRS 20110016041](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110016041/downloads/20110016041.pdf)). The habitat ground rules carry a **10% food margin for emergency** ([DRA Addendum ~p.276](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)).

Greenhouse sizing comes from BVAD. Edible yields, averaged over a crop's whole life cycle, run from **105 g/m²·d fresh for white potato**, 131 for lettuce and 174 for tomato, down to just 5 for soybean ([BVAD Rev2 Table 4-90](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855)). Required growing area per crewmember is **1.35 m²** for a salad-only diet (0.14 kg/d of fresh crops). It is **19.5 m²** for salad plus carbohydrates, about half the food mass. It is **65.3 m²** for an all-crop diet. Crew aisles add 25–50% on top ([BVAD Rev2 Tables 4-58, 4-92, p.170](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855)). Nothing is harvested until a crop's first growth period ends: **25–30 days** for greens, **85 days** for sweet potato, **132 days** for white potato. NASA staggers planting so output stays steady. The 2024 KSC design runs three salad groups 14 days apart, and a JSC test replaced 25% of the wheat area every 20 days ([BVAD Table 4-89](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855); [Space Crops TM 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001897/downloads/20231215%20Space%20Crops%20HRP%20FINAL%20STRIVES%203.pdf); [MELiSSA summary](https://www.melissafoundation.org/download/427)).

Hardware is heavy. BVAD's reference chamber weighs **101.5 kg per m²**, including 36.8 kg of water and nutrients, and its HPS-era lighting draws **2.6 kW/m²**, with the same amount of heat to reject. BVAD notes those lamp figures have not been updated for LEDs ([BVAD Rev2 Table 4-88](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855)). Modern LEDs reach 3.4–4.1 µmol/J ([Kusuma et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7105460/)). Dividing each crop's light need by that efficacy (my estimate, not a NASA figure) gives about **80 W/m² for lettuce, 135 W/m² for potato or soy, and 555 W/m² for wheat**, averaged over the day. A full-diet greenhouse would then need ~9 kW per crewmember, roughly one Kilopower unit per person. Natural sunlight reaches up to 30 mol/m²·d at good latitudes, enough for potato, but it drops in winter and in storms, so a sunlit greenhouse still needs electric backup ([BVAD Rev2 p.168](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210024855)).

The verdict on mass is consistent across studies. Physicochemical life support with stored food is the cheapest option for the missions studied. Crops pay off as salad "for any mission" (for nutrition and morale), as staples only for medium-length missions, and as most of the food only for missions of about a decade ([Drysdale, Ewert & Hanford 2003](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040087862)). A NASA-BioSim study puts the Mars-surface crossover at **≥4.8 years** with current technology. Cheaper lighting and more automation could cut that by more than half ([Shaw 2014, MIT](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/90797)). NASA's 2024 crop memo admits that a full trade "has not previously been done" ([Space Crops TM 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001897/downloads/20231215%20Space%20Crops%20HRP%20FINAL%20STRIVES%203.pdf)). On reserves against crop failure, NASA gives no numeric rule. It recommends **at least three crop families** and says crops must first prove "low risk of crop loss" before the crew depends on them. That caution has a real precedent: in 2015, ISS Veggie zinnias were lost to *Fusarium* after poor airflow ([Space Crops TM](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001897/downloads/20231215%20Space%20Crops%20HRP%20FINAL%20STRIVES%203.pdf); [Schuerger 2021](https://plantpath.ifas.ufl.edu/misc/media/Schuerger_2021_Astrobiology_F_oxy.pdf)).

## Water and oxygen are recycled and made, not shipped

NASA's mass balance makes water the heaviest commodity: "By mass, H2O is the most burdensome commodity." A 4-crew habitat takes in 17.1 kg/day before recycling ([DRA Addendum §7](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)). BVAD sets potable water at **2.5 kg/CM-d**. Total water use is **4.48 kg/CM-d for an early planetary base** with showers, 9.82 for a mature base, and **4.0 kg/m²·d for plant growth** ([BVAD Rev1 Table 4-20](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20180001338)). ISS reached **98% total water recovery** in 2023 after adding the Brine Processor; before that it was 93–94% ([NASA, June 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/nasa-achieves-water-recovery-milestone-on-international-space-station/)). At 4.48 kg/CM-d for 4 crew, that is a loss of about **0.36 kg/day at 98%**, against 1.16 kg/day at 93.5% (my arithmetic). Over a long stay that difference is several hundred kilograms. DRA 5.0 does not ship bulk water. It lands **400 kg of hydrogen** and reacts it with ISRU oxygen to replace water lost in crew and EVA use. ISRU also collects nitrogen and argon buffer gas from the atmosphere ([DRA 5.0 pp.36, 40](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf)). A ground rule requires all ISRU-made consumables to be finished before the crew leaves Earth ([Addendum §3.6.6](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)).

Oxygen needs are **0.84 kg/CM-d on the surface**, plus about 0.005 kg/day of cabin leakage per module ([ASCEND 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230013896/downloads/Logistics_BeyondLEO-ASCEND2023-Pres.pdf)). It comes from electrolysing recycled water. The ISS Oxygen Generation Assembly makes up to 9.2 kg/day at **3,955 W**, which works out to about 10 kWh per kg of O2 (derived). A 4-crew exploration unit needs only ~4.4 kg/day ([ICES-2015-115](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150016494/downloads/20150016494.pdf)). Each kilogram of O2 consumes 1.125 kg of water, which links the two loops in a game model. Breathing is a small load next to propellant: 4 crew use ~3.4 kg O2/day, while a full-scale MOXIE-derived plant would make 2–3 kg/h. MOXIE itself made 6–12 g/h on Mars ([Hoffman et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432831/)). NASA's water-ISRU studies assume **~16 t of water per crew**, mainly for ascent oxygen ([M-WIP](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2020/04/Mars_Water_ISRU_Stud1.pdf)).

## Reserves live in orbit, in duplicates, and in mass margins

NASA's Mars reserve takes the form of duplicate hardware and stock, not a set number of days. The crewed transit vehicle carries **2,650 kg** of outbound food, 2,650 kg of return food and **7,940 kg of contingency food**. That contingency stock is about 540 days for 6 crew, the full surface stay. If the surface mission is aborted, the orbiting vehicle serves as a safe haven until the return window opens ([DRA 5.0 Table 4-4](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf); [Addendum §5](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)). The surface system uses redundant pairs. It has two ISRU plants, "each sized to generate the needed consumables", and a spare Kilopower to switch on if one fails. Habitable volumes are redundant, each able to hold the whole crew, and the rovers back up the habitat's life support for about two weeks ([DRA 5.0 p.40](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf); [Rucker 2016](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160010550); [Addendum](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)). Mass margins are **15% growth on Kilopower, 20% on DRA power and habitat, and 30% on transit-habitat dry mass and solar**. The ISS equivalent for consumables is the **45-day "skip cycle"**, enough to survive one missed cargo flight ([NTRS 20060056067](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20060056067/downloads/20060056067.pdf); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/590-iss-crew-counts-calories-food-supply-runs.html)). I found no NASA rule for a fixed number of reserve sols of water or oxygen on the Mars surface, so a game should not claim one.

## Making the game's single 40 t lander feasible

The game's fixed items already take most of the lander. The 21.5 t habitat, 3 × 1,754 kg Kilopower and 2 × ~1,678 kg ISRU plants total **30.1 t**, leaving **9.88 t**. At 2.39 kg/CM-d for 4 crew that buys **~1,034 days of food**. A full 1,319-day stay needs **12.6 t**, and a 2,099-day stay needs **20.1 t**, before NASA's 10% margin. The deficit is real, and NASA's architecture already contains the fixes. All figures in the table are my arithmetic on the cited inputs. One sol is ~1.0275 Earth days ([Appelbaum & Flood](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890018252)), so sol 804 is about day 826.

The ship carries the remaining 493 days (4.7 t) for the 1,319-day stay. | Pre-deployed follow-on cargo is part of the plan and a backup; 10% food margin ([DRA 5.0 p.51](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373665main_NASA-SP-2009-566.pdf); [Addendum](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/373667main_NASA-SP-2009-566-ADD.pdf)) |

Some fixes should not be made. Growing food to save mass does not work on the first lander. A salad plot of 5.4 m² for 4 crew weighs ~550 kg and draws ~0.5–0.7 kW. It earns its place through morale and vitamins, not mass. A half-diet greenhouse of 78 m² weighs ~7.9 t, needs ~10.5 kW of LED light (more than one extra Kilopower) and breaks even only after ~2,200 days. A 2,099-day stay is just short of that, so a staple greenhouse belongs on the second ship. Model it in the game as `yield = area × g/m²·d` after a first-harvest delay (132 days for potato), with staggered groups and at least three crop families. Keep the full stored-food backup until the greenhouse has shown it can produce. Water should not be shipped in bulk either. Model it as a loop with 93.5% recovery, rising to 98% with a brine-processor upgrade, topped up by ISRU. The 3,168 kg battery and 2,034 kg solar sets are worth carrying only as storm-tolerant backup to fission, sized for a 120-sol storm at ~30% light.

## Conclusion

The game's impasse comes from treating one lander as the entire mission. In NASA's plans, each expedition is a pipeline. Power and supplies land first, the crew arrives on the habitat lander, the next crew's cargo lands before the first crew leaves, and the abort reserve stays in orbit. The game's ship at sol 742–804 already gives it that pipeline. Counting that ship as resupply, and adding NASA's 10% margin, makes the current loadout fit with about 1.2 t to spare. No numbers need changing.

The best gameplay tensions also come from NASA's own tradeoffs. Fission against solar is a choice of dust-storm risk against reactor mass. A greenhouse costs kilowatts and kilograms and pays back only after years. The brine-processor upgrade cuts water losses about threefold. A fifth Kilopower or a second ISRU plant buys redundancy at the cost of food. Two numbers are not in NASA's literature: a fixed surface reserve in sols, and the power of an LED-era greenhouse. The game should label any values it uses for those as design choices, not NASA figures.
